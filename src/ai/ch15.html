Part VIII · Architecture and leadership

Making and recording decisions

A good architecture decision starts from measurable requirements, compares real options including the simplest one, states its trade-offs plainly, and names the measurable trigger that would reopen it; writing it down is what lets the next person trust it or change it.

At a glance
  • Think it through in order: problem, constraints, requirements, options, decision, trade-offs, risks, operations, and when to revisit.
  • A PRD says what and why; an RFC proposes how and gathers review before building; an ADR records a decision made, kept forever and superseded rather than edited.
  • What decides most choices: the requirements, the team's ability to run it, the cost at the expected scale, and how hard it is to reverse.
  • Cheap-to-reverse decisions can be made fast; expensive ones deserve an RFC.
  • Each recurring AI choice has a default and evidence that flips it.

How to say it

"I'd write it as a decision record: the measurable requirements, two real options including the simplest, the trade-offs, how we'd run it, and the trigger that would make us revisit it."

"Our default is pgvector in Postgres; we'd move to a vector database at hundreds of millions of vectors or a filtering need it can't serve, and we'd measure that first."

Questions

  1. Answers must come from 40 million document clauses, filtered by client and jurisdiction, in under two seconds. pgvector or a vector database, and what do you measure first?

    Answer

    Start from pgvector unless a measurement rules it out. First measure filtered recall and latency at that scale, with realistic filter selectivity (the overfiltering trap is the risk for small clients), and index memory. Move to a dedicated store, or partition per client, if the filtered queries cannot meet two seconds with acceptable recall.

  2. Summarizing a 300-page filing takes four minutes. What does the API return, where does the work run, and how does the user get the result?

    Answer

    The API returns 202 with a job id at once; a worker behind a queue does the work with a lease, retries and a dead-letter queue; the user polls the job or is notified (a webhook, an event, a message) when it is done.

  3. A client's data may not leave the EU, and they want an AI feature in six weeks. Managed API in an EU region, or self-hosted?

    Answer

    A managed API in an EU region, if its terms guarantee processing and retention in the region and no training on the data; six weeks rules out building a serving stack. Self-hosting becomes the answer if no provider's terms satisfy the requirement, or sustained load would keep GPUs busy.

  4. Product asks for an agent that handles support tickets. When would you ship a workflow instead?

    Answer

    When the steps are known (classify, look up, draft a reply for a person), when results must be predictable and auditable, or when cost and latency matter. Switch to an agent only if evals on real tickets show it handles cases the workflow cannot, at an acceptable cost.
