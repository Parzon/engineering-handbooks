Part VIII · Architecture and leadership

Leading AI delivery

Delivering an AI feature is ordinary engineering leadership with one extra risk at its center: whether the model can do the task well enough is unknown until evals say so, so that assumption is tested first and every plan, estimate and conversation is built around it.

At a glance
  • Turn a vague ask into an outcome, a user and a metric; write down constraints and non-goals; test the riskiest assumption first.
  • Order the risks: can it work (quality on real data), can it run (deploy, permissions, approvals), can it be operated (monitoring, rollback).
  • Estimate in ranges with assumptions; "works in the demo" to "passes the evals" is often the longest part.
  • Under schedule pressure, cut autonomy and scope before quality; never cut access control, isolation, the audit trail, the eval gate or the off switch.
  • Status is progress against the plan; bad news early, with a plan.

From a vague ask to slices with owners

A twelve-week shape

Estimates, risk and debt

  • Estimate in ranges, with assumptions: "three to five weeks if the documents are clean text already". Break work into pieces of days; list dependencies (access, data, other teams, security review, procurement); name the unknowns. When an assumption breaks, re-estimate and say so early.
  • The AI-specific slip is the gap between a demo that works on chosen examples and a feature that passes the evals on real ones; budget for it explicitly.
  • A short risk list (likelihood, impact, owner, mitigation, and the signal that it is happening), reviewed with the status.
  • Technical debt is a loan: take it deliberately and write down what and why; repay it when it slows the team or threatens reliability; never take it in access control, data isolation or evals.
  • Time-box research with a question and an exit criterion; promote to production only what passed the evals; and budget the hardening, which is most of the work.

Standards, review and the team

PracticeWhat good looks like
StandardsThey live in the repository (agent instructions, CI checks, templates), not in heads; automate what can be checked, review what cannot
ReviewFor correctness, clarity and shared ownership; say what and why, mark blocking or optional; review a design early rather than the code late

StoppingRe-scope or stop when the riskiest assumption failed, cost outweighs value, or evals cannot reach the bar; eval results make that call defensible

Incidents follow the blameless pattern of any service (Handbook 2, Chapter 17), with two additions for AI: record the prompt, model and data versions in the timeline, and add the failure to the eval set so it cannot return unnoticed.

How to say it

"The first thing we test is whether the model can do the task on real data, with a small eval set; everything else is planned around that answer."

"If the date is fixed, we cut autonomy and scope, not access control, isolation, the audit trail, the eval gate or the off switch."

Questions

  1. What is usually the riskiest assumption in an AI project, and how is it tested?

    Answer

    That the model can do the task well enough on real data. It is tested first, with a short spike and a small eval set of realistic cases, before anything is built around it.

  2. Product wants an agent in three weeks; engineering estimates eight. What do you do?

    Answer

    Ask what the date is for and what must work on day one; cut autonomy and scope (a workflow or read-only tools, one use case, one data source, internal users), ship the narrowest end-to-end slice behind a flag with a person approving any action, and state the risks. Do not cut access control, isolation, the audit trail, the safety eval gate or the off switch.

  3. Where does AI work usually slip against an estimate?

    Answer

    Between "works in the demo" and "passes the evals on real data", and in approvals with long lead times: security review, data-processing terms, model access.
